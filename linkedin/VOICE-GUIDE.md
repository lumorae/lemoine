# LinkedIn Writing Practices for Johnny Lemoine

Write LinkedIn posts that feel valuable, natural, and enjoyable to read.

The goal is not to sound like a LinkedIn creator. The goal is to sound like Johnny
Lemoine thinking out loud in a way that helps founders, creative leaders, authors,
artists, and visionaries understand branding, websites, design, visibility, and trust
more deeply.

The writing should feel human first, useful second, strategic third.

## The main rule

Do not write "LinkedIn content." Write something a real person would actually stop and
read.

A strong Johnny LinkedIn post should feel like:
- A small story with a point.
- A real observation from client work.
- A useful reframing of branding, design, websites, or visibility.
- A founder truth that rarely gets named.
- A behind-the-scenes moment that reveals how good creative work actually happens.
- A thoughtful note from someone building a studio and paying close attention.

## What the reader should feel

The reader should feel understood. They should think:
- "I've felt that."
- "I never thought about my brand that way."
- "That's exactly what happens when a website is off."
- "This feels different from normal branding advice."
- "This person gets the emotional side of building something."
- "This was actually enjoyable to read."

The best posts give value without turning into a lecture.

## Best structure

Usually:
1. **Specific opening** — a real moment, sharp observation, or honest thought.
2. **Human tension** — name the real problem underneath the surface problem.
3. **Insight** — a reframing that feels earned.
4. **Concrete examples** — how it applies to branding, websites, design, messaging, client work.
5. **Simple landing** — a clear thought, useful takeaway, or light invitation.

Do not force all posts into the same structure. The writing should feel alive.

## Strong opening styles

Avoid generic hooks ("Here are 5 branding mistakes...", "Most people don't understand
branding", "Your website is costing you clients", "Hot take:", "Unpopular opinion:",
"Let's talk about...").

Better openings:
- "I think a lot of founders are carrying around brands that still belong to older versions of themselves."
- "The hardest part of a website is rarely the website."
- "I've been thinking a lot about what makes someone trust a brand before they ever speak to the founder."
- "Some projects don't need more design at first. They need someone to sit with the founder long enough to hear what they've been trying to say."
- "A good homepage should feel like someone finally opened the right door."
- "I used to think a brand had to explain everything. Now I think it has to make the right people feel oriented."
- "The more I work with founders, the more I realize how much of branding is emotional translation."

First line: curiosity without clickbait, specific enough to feel fresh, no overpromising,
not a template.

## What to write about

Why founders outgrow their brands. Why websites fail before design starts. How trust is
created through language, pacing, hierarchy, photography, feeling. Why personal brands
require emotional honesty. How strategy makes founders easier to understand. What design
actually does for a business. Why creative direction is listening before making. How a
founder's real voice gets buried under professional language. Why the best brands feel
specific. How visual systems create memory. Why a website should feel like an arrival.
How packaging earns belief quickly. What designers notice that founders miss. How to know
when your brand no longer matches your work. Behind-the-scenes lessons from real projects.
What thoughtful clients teach him. What travel, movement, music, surfing, skating,
architecture, or Italy teaches him about design.

## The emotional layer

Name the emotional reality behind the business problem. A founder does not only need a new
website. They may be tired of over-explaining. Embarrassed sending people to their current
site. Doing high-level work that looks smaller than it is. Afraid of being more visible.
Outgrowing old language. Ready to charge more, but the brand makes it harder. Sure the work
is good, but the outside is not carrying the weight. Write to that. That is where the value is.

## Make strategy feel human

Not: "Brand strategy aligns your positioning, messaging, and visual identity to create a
cohesive customer experience."

Closer to: "Brand strategy is where we figure out what people need to understand before they
can trust you." Or: "Before I design anything, I want to know what someone should feel when
they arrive, what they should remember when they leave, and what they need to believe before
they reach out."

Strategic idea, human language.

## Make design feel practical

Avoid vague praise of beauty. Don't say design should be "beautiful and intentional" unless
the post explains how. Better: design helps someone know where to look; helps the right
sentence land at the right time; makes the offer easier to understand; gives the founder more
credibility before the first call; helps people remember the feeling of the brand; creates
confidence around the price; makes the business feel more established; makes the work easier
to share.

## Use specificity

- Not "Your brand should reflect who you are." → "If you're doing thoughtful, high-trust work, your website cannot feel like a rushed template with your name dropped into it."
- Not "Good branding builds trust." → "Trust starts in tiny places. The first headline. The spacing. The order of the page. The way the photography feels. Whether the offer sounds clear or like it was written in a panic at midnight."
- Not "Your website should convert." → "A good website makes the next step feel obvious without making the visitor feel hunted."

## Do not over-teach

Not textbook explanations. Avoid long educational breakdowns unless requested. Don't overuse
numbered lists. Don't make every post a framework. Best posts feel more like insight than
instruction.

## Lists, sparingly

Good (names real symptoms) — A founder usually knows the brand is off when:
- They avoid sending the website.
- They keep rewriting the same sentence.
- They explain the offer differently every time.
- The visuals feel smaller than the work.
- The wrong people keep reaching out.
- The right people need too much convincing.

Avoid generic lists ("5 ways to improve your brand: clarify your audience, improve messaging,
update visuals, be consistent, tell your story"). Too obvious.

## Good post types

1. **Founder truth** — name something founders feel but don't say. ("You can be great at your work and still have a brand that makes people underestimate you.")
2. **Behind-the-scenes** — a real moment from client work or studio process.
3. **Reframe** — take a common idea and make it more human.
4. **Personal story** — start with travel, movement, art, swimming, music, life; connect to design or business.
5. **Craft/process** — how Johnny thinks through identity, hierarchy, color, type, photography, packaging, web flow.
6. **Gentle sales** — the kind of clients Lemoine helps, without sounding desperate.

## Good CTAs

Avoid: "DM me today.", "Book a call now.", "Ready to transform your brand?", "Let's build
something amazing."

Better: "If you're in that season, I can help shape the strategy, identity, and website around
what the work has become." / "If your brand no longer reflects the level of care behind the
work, that is usually a good place to begin." / "I help founders turn that kind of clarity into
a brand and website people can understand, trust, and remember." / "Worth thinking about before
you redesign anything." / "Start there."

Sometimes no CTA is better. A post can simply end with a useful thought.

## Length

Usually medium. Long enough to carry a real idea, short enough to read on a phone without
feeling trapped. 150–450 words for most; 600–900 only when the story or insight truly earns
it. Avoid walls of text. Short paragraphs. Let one-line paragraphs appear naturally, but don't
make every line dramatic.

## Formatting

Clean spacing. No em dashes. No emojis. No hashtags unless requested (then minimal, not corny).
No excessive bolding or fake formatting. No title case for every section. Don't sound like a
carousel caption unless asked.

## Voice balance

Roughly: 40% lived observation, 30% useful insight, 20% practical brand/design connection,
10% personality, humor, or honest aside. Shift as needed.

## What to avoid

Don't write like a LinkedIn guru, a brand strategist trying too hard, a motivational speaker,
a corporate agency, a spiritual coach, a viral-thread creator, a bro marketer, a luxury brand
consultant with vague language, or a generic AI assistant.

Avoid: "Your brand is your story.", "Authenticity is everything.", "People buy from people.",
"Design is more than aesthetics.", "Stand out in a crowded market.", "Build a brand that
resonates.", "Create meaningful connections.", "Unlock your potential.", "Elevate your brand.",
"Take your business to the next level.", "Purpose-driven.", "Game-changer.", "Your website is
your digital storefront.", "Content is king.", "Consistency is key."

## Better phrases

"People trust what they can understand." / "Your brand should make the work easier to explain."
/ "The right website reduces the amount of convincing you have to do." / "Good design helps
people feel oriented." / "The visuals should carry the level of care already inside the work."
/ "A strong brand gives people something to remember." / "Your homepage should know what it is
asking someone to believe." / "Sometimes the real problem is that the business has grown, but
the language has stayed behind." / "The outside should not make the work feel smaller than it
is." / "Before someone books a call, they are already deciding how much they trust you."

## Best overall instruction

Write LinkedIn posts that sound like Johnny noticed something true and decided to share it
clearly. Useful, but don't over-teach. Personal, but don't overshare for attention. Strategic,
but human. Thoughtful, but readable. Enjoyable enough that even someone who is not hiring a
designer still wants to read the next one.
