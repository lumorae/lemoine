how i work.

Written for SOMA Laboratory · July 2026

You asked how I approach a project. Here's the real answer.

You've spent years building a world — the instruments, the essays, a LYRA you learn with your hands instead of a manual. I wouldn't be inventing a brand for you. I'd be making sure that world survives the trip to a screen.

Who's writing this

I was an architect for fifteen years before this studio. Architecture teaches you to hold one large idea and a thousand small decisions at once, and to make the smallest detail belong to the whole. That habit is most of my method.

I'm also a musician, which is probably why I keep ending up next to instrument makers. I know what it's like to want an object you can't get your hands on yet. Brand, identity, web, packaging, custom type — usually together, because the idea leaks when it passes between people.

The process

01

I learn the whole world

The essays, the interviews, the performances, the site you have now. Then the unglamorous half: how dealers work, what customers ask before they commit, what your team updates every week, where the site makes work instead of saving it. Done when I can explain SOMA back to you in your own words and you recognize it.

02

I find the one idea

Every project I've made has a single idea at its center, and everything after answers to it. Yours would need to be precise enough to decide with and wide enough to hold everything you make. Once it exists we stop arguing about taste — a choice either serves the idea or it doesn't, and we can both see which.

03

I build the visual language

Type, color, composition, image, motion. Drawn by hand when that's what the idea asks for — for High Spirits I ended up drawing them a whole typeface. Enough discipline to hold together, enough air to stay alive, and the reasoning attached so your team keeps it right after I'm gone.

04

I design the site as a place

This is the architecture showing. A website is a space: someone enters, sees something first, goes deeper or leaves. One person meets SOMA through LYRA-8, another through an interview with Vlad, another through a dealer page three levels down. Every one of those is a front door. I map the structure first, then build the key pages as prototypes we can walk through before it's built.

05

I build it, and I stay

I write the code myself, so there's no handoff and nothing gets lost in one. It's built to grow — new instruments, new writing, new languages — without losing its character. I stay on for a stretch after launch, and I'll tell you when a decision weakens the idea. Mine included.

The closest thing I've made to this

High Spirits Flutes

Native American flutes, made by hand in Arizona for decades. Same problem as yours: the instrument is the whole argument, and nobody holds one before they buy.

I did the strategy, the identity, a custom typeface drawn from the hand-painted American signage they love, and the store itself. First I learned how the flutes are made, what the keys mean, and how people choose one. That's what the site is made of.

Where I'd start with SOMA

Questions, not proposals:

  1. What should someone understand about SOMA after ten minutes with one of the instruments?
  2. Where does the site lose people right now?
  3. What must never change — and what's ready to?
  4. What does your team need it to make easier every week?

None of this is a pitch for right now. You said the timing isn't there and I believe you. I'd just rather be someone you already know when it changes.
